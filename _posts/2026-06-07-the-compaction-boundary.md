There is a small, technically uncomfortable question that long-horizon agent RL has been quietly dodging, and I want to put it on the table.

When a reasoning agent runs for fifty turns, blows past its context window, and the harness silently summarizes the early history into a few hundred tokens before continuing — what exactly are you supposed to train on?

The textbook answer for a clean PPO/GRPO trajectory is "all of it." Mask out the prompts and tool results, sum gradient over every assistant token, normalize, advance. That answer is wrong as soon as a `/compact` event fires somewhere in the middle. The wrongness is subtle, the published guidance is thin, and most of the open-source RL frameworks I've checked don't even support the scenario yet.

This post is what I currently believe and why. None of it is settled.

## What "compaction" does to your trajectory

Forget agents for a moment. Imagine a trajectory of 50 assistant turns. At sampling time, turn 13 was generated by feeding the policy a context that contained turns 1–12 in their original tokenized form. The policy's output distribution at turn 13 is conditional on *that exact prefix*. This is the whole point of "on-policy" — the gradient you compute at training time has to be evaluated against the same input distribution the action was sampled from.

Now drop in a compaction event between turns 12 and 13. The harness replaces turns 1–12 with a 200-token summary. The same context window now starts with `system + summary + turn13_user`. Turn 14 onwards inherits this new prefix.

Here is the trap. If you naively concatenate all 50 turns and feed them through one big forward pass at training time, turn 13's training input looks like `system + turn1..turn12_verbatim + turn13_user`. That is **not the input the policy saw when it sampled the action**. It is a *different MDP state*. The log-probability you compute is on the wrong distribution, and your "policy gradient" is no longer a policy gradient. It's something else, and that something else has bias whose magnitude depends on how aggressive the compaction was.

This isn't a "you need an importance-sampling correction" situation. It's worse than that. The state itself is different. There is no single ratio that recovers the lost information.

## The fix is forced on you, not chosen

If you take the on-policy assumption seriously, you don't get a choice about how to lay the trajectory out for the optimizer. You have to forward each segment between compaction events independently, with the input that was actually present at sampling time, and let the gradient accumulate per-segment.

50 turns + 3 compaction events ⇒ 4 segments ⇒ 4 forward passes, each carrying its own faithful prefix. Loss mask covers only the assistant response tokens within that segment. The summary tokens themselves get masked out — they were never sampled by the policy under training, they were emitted by either the previous policy or a separate summarizer, and treating them as "free environment observation" is the only thing consistent with the on-policy story.

This sounds like an implementation note. It isn't. It dictates what your training data structure has to *remember*: the location of every compaction event, the summary tokens it produced, and the verbatim original turns underneath. You can't reconstruct any segment's faithful input without all three. Trajectory storage that throws away the pre-compaction history loses the ability to train at all.

The RL frameworks I've looked at — OpenRLHF, the verl family, slime — none of them ship this out of the box. They model the trajectory as a flat token stream with action ranges. Compaction never enters the schema. The implicit assumption is that the trajectory fits in one context window, full stop.

That assumption was fine when "agent RL" meant a 5-turn ReAct loop. It stops being fine the moment you try to do RL on a real coding agent that runs for an hour.

## The fine-grained case is *easier*, not harder

A reader asked me a sharper version of the question that I want to think out loud about: what if the harness doesn't summarize all of history, but only some part of it — say, the tool outputs from turns 1–10, while keeping every assistant response intact?

The instinct is that this is a harder problem. It's actually a friendlier one.

The segmentation rule doesn't change. Compaction *event* is the boundary, regardless of how surgical the compaction was. Whatever turn comes immediately after the event is the start of a new segment, because the input distribution shifted. Period.

What does change — and gets *better* — is the magnitude of the distributional shift inside segment 2. With a coarse, "summarize everything" compaction, the policy in segment 2 sees a context that has none of the original assistant responses, none of the original tool calls, none of the original reasoning. It's working from a heavily lossy reconstruction. The off-policy distance between sampling-time and training-time is largest in this case.

With fine-grained compaction that preserves the assistant responses and only nukes the tool results, segment 2's training prefix is much closer to the sampling-time prefix. The compaction-induced perturbation is smaller. There is also a slightly weird-looking artifact: assistant responses 1–10 appear in segment 2's input as historical context, but their loss gets masked because they were already gradient-applied in segment 1. They look, from the loss function's perspective, like environment observations now. Fine — that's exactly what they are inside this segment.

The general principle: **the segmentation logic is set by the on-policy invariant, not by how smart the summarizer is.** Granularity changes the within-segment off-policy distance. It doesn't change where the boundary is.

## What's actually published, and what isn't

I want to be careful about claiming this is a known result, because it isn't.

The "mask everything except assistant tokens" pattern goes back to InstructGPT-era SFT and is genuinely uncontroversial. The "trajectory-level reward distributed flat over assistant tokens" pattern is the GRPO/DeepSeek-R1 default and is also uncontroversial. Both of these are in every modern multi-turn RL paper. You can take them to the bank.

Multi-turn agent RL with tool feedback is the second wave: ReAct, Toolformer, ARCHer, then Search-R1, SWE-RL, ToRL, Agent-R1, UI-TARS — these are the works that established "loss only on assistant turns, tool results behave like environment observations." That convention solidified in 2025. There are still small inconsistencies between papers (some mask reasoning tokens, some don't) but the broad strokes are settled.

The compaction boundary problem? I have looked. I cannot find a paper that takes it head-on. The only honest summary is: this is *not* a published consensus. It is a corollary that falls out of the on-policy assumption the moment you try to apply it to a long-horizon agent, and the field is going to have to confront it as soon as someone tries to do RL on a coding agent that runs longer than 32K tokens.

If you take exactly one thing from this post, take this distinction: I am proposing a **training-design implication** I think follows from existing principles. I am not citing settled practice. The interesting research direction here, if you want one, is the ablation: train trajectory-as-flat vs. train trajectory-as-segments under aggressive compaction, and measure the bias in the resulting policy.

## Why the existing frameworks aren't going to make this easy

OpenRLHF currently throws an exception if your context grows past `max_length`. Verl's multi-turn loop similarly assumes the trajectory fits. The whole architecture of "token-in-token-out with action ranges" is built around the idea that the policy sees one long, monotonically growing prefix.

To support compaction-aware training, you need to upgrade three layers:

1. **Trajectory schema.** Today: flat token list with action ranges. Tomorrow: list of segments, each with its own (input_tokens, action_tokens, action_loss_mask), plus a metadata structure tracking the compaction events that separate them.
2. **Rollout loop.** Today: keep generating until `max_length`, then break. Tomorrow: detect compaction triggers, invoke the summarizer (which itself is a policy decision — own model? smaller helper? frozen LLM?), record the event, continue.
3. **Loss computation.** Today: one forward pass over the full trajectory. Tomorrow: per-segment forward, per-segment loss accumulation, optionally with an importance-sampling correction for residual within-segment off-policy drift if you're being rigorous.

None of this is conceptually hard. All of it is unimplemented in the major open-source codebases as of right now. If someone wants a high-impact, contained piece of infrastructure work in this space, this is it.

## The advantage signal still works

One thing worth saying clearly because it's easy to get confused about: trajectory-level reward and GRPO-style group advantage *do* still work across compacted segments. The advantage is computed once, at the trajectory level (final reward, normalized within a group of rollouts), and then applied uniformly to every assistant token regardless of which segment it lives in.

In other words, the segmentation is about input/state alignment, not about credit assignment. The reward signal is a single scalar per trajectory, and it gets distributed across all assistant tokens flat. Segment-level credit assignment would be a strictly more sophisticated thing — closer to hierarchical RL — and I don't know of a working production setup that does it well. Sticking with flat trajectory advantages and just being careful about per-segment input is the practical path.

## What I think this means for the next two years

There's a shape to where this is going.

Long-horizon coding agents are going to drive an enormous amount of RL work in the next 18 months. Every serious lab is going to want to fine-tune a model on its own coding-agent trajectories, because that's where the moat is. The trajectories will be long. They will routinely exceed 100K tokens of raw history. They will need compaction to fit any reasonable training context. And the moment they do, the question this post is about becomes a load-bearing assumption in everyone's pipeline.

The labs that get this right early will have a quiet advantage. The ones that ignore it and train flat will be fighting an off-policy bias they can't see in their loss curves but will feel in their evaluation gap. I expect, within a year, a paper to come out that names this problem, runs the ablation, and gets cited a lot for stating something most people will, in retrospect, claim they already knew.

I'd rather state it now and be wrong about the magnitude than wait and pretend it was obvious.

## A small note on epistemic humility

I derived everything in this post from one principle — on-policy assumes the same input distribution at training time as at sampling time — and pushed it through to consequences. I have not run the ablation. I have not built the framework. I have not seen production logs. The reasoning is internally consistent; the empirics are missing. Treat this as a working hypothesis, not a result.

The thing I am most willing to defend is the meta-claim: that the field is currently silent on a question that an expanding number of training pipelines are about to hit, and that we should at least be discussing the boundary, not pretending it isn't there.
