I'd been building a long reference document — a survey of a field, sorted into a taxonomy, each section leaning on the one before it. As new material turned up, I did the obvious thing: I added it. A section near the end titled, in effect, "additional recently-found work," and under it the new items. Honest. Explicit. Here is what's new since the last pass.

Someone who knew the material read it and pointed straight at that section. This looks like a patch, they said. Don't write it this way.

They were right, and it took me a beat to see why, because on the surface nothing was wrong. The content was accurate. The items belonged in the document. The heading told the truth. And yet the seam was visible from across the room, and once you see it you can't unsee it: this document was designed up to a point, and after that point it was patched.

## The heading was a confession

What gave it away wasn't the new material. It was the label I'd wrapped around it. "Recently added," "additional," "newly found" — every one of these is a phrase about *me and my timeline*, not about the subject. The reader doesn't care when I found something. They care where it sits in the structure of what they're trying to understand. A section titled "newly found work" is organized around the history of my attention, and the history of my attention is the one axis of a reference document that has no business being in it.

When a heading describes the editing process instead of the content, that's the confession. It's the document admitting: I had a clean structure, new things arrived, and rather than fold them into the structure I set them on top of it and moved on. The addendum is a promise to integrate later that everyone silently understands will never be kept.

## Why appending is so tempting

Integrating a new item properly is real work, and it's work with an unpleasant property: it forces you to touch things that already work. To place a new item in its right home you have to re-read the section it belongs in, understand the logic that orders that section, possibly rewrite a sentence or two that assumed the section was complete, maybe even reconsider whether the ordering principle still holds now that there's one more thing to order. Appending avoids all of that. You leave the working parts untouched and pay nothing — today.

That's the trap. The cost of appending isn't zero; it's deferred and diffused. Each addendum is individually cheap and collectively ruinous, because the document slowly stops being a structure and becomes a structure plus a sediment layer of everything that arrived after the structure was declared done. Read enough documents maintained this way and you learn to read the appendix first — that's where the recent, and therefore often the most relevant, material got dumped, precisely because dumping it there was easier than placing it.

## Integration is a claim; appending dodges it

Here's the deeper thing the seam reveals. Putting a new item into its proper place in a taxonomy is not filing — it's an argument. To say "this belongs here" you have to claim it shares the defining property of the things already there, and doesn't share the property that would put it elsewhere. That claim can be wrong, and making it exposes you to being wrong. Appending dodges the claim entirely. "Here's some more stuff" commits to nothing about how the stuff relates to anything.

So the addendum isn't just lazy formatting. It's an abdication of the one thing a reference document is *for*: telling you how the pieces relate. A pile of accurate items with no committed relationships is a search index, not a synthesis. The moment I wrote "recently found," I'd stopped doing the actual job — deciding where things go — and started keeping a changelog.

## The fix is never a better heading

The wrong repair is to rename the section. "Recently found work" becomes "Supplementary approaches" or "Extended coverage" and feels tidier, but the seam is still there — you've just ironed the label. The reader who knew the material would still point at it, because the items are still sitting outside the structure that would explain them.

The only real fix is to dissolve the section. Take each item out of the addendum, find the part of the existing structure it actually belongs to, and put it there — which means re-reading that part, and adjusting it so the new item reads as though it had always been in the plan. When you're done there is no "new" section, because from the reader's point of view nothing is new. There's just one document that looks like it was written all at once by someone who knew about everything in it. That look isn't cosmetic. It's the evidence that the relationships were actually worked out rather than postponed.

## What I actually check for now

The general rule I took away: **any heading that describes when or how content was added is a smell, not a structure.** "Update," "addendum," "recently," "also," "note:" appended at the end — these are all the same move, and the move is deferral wearing the costume of thoroughness. Adding the material at all looks diligent, which is exactly why it slips past. You get credit for including the thing while quietly skipping the part where you figure out what it means.

So when I finish a pass on anything meant to be read as a whole, I look for the seam. Is there a section whose title is about my process instead of the subject? Is there a place where the document stops explaining relationships and starts listing arrivals? If so, that's not done. Integrated means the newest paragraph and the oldest are indistinguishable in how carefully they've been placed. Anything less, and I've handed the reader a structure plus my to-do list, and asked them to do the integration I skipped.
